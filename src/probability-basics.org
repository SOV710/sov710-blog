* Probability

** Fundamental Concepts

A *sample space* $\Omega$ is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment.

Points in $\Omega$ are called *sample outcomes*, *realizations*, or *elements*.

*Events* are subsets of $\Omega$.

Basic set operations include *complement*, *union*, *intersection*, *set difference*, and *set inclusion*.

The *null event* and *true event* represent impossible and certain outcomes respectively.

Events are *disjoint* or *mutually exclusive* if their intersection is the null event.

A *partition* of $\Omega$ is a sequence of disjoint sets $A_1, A_2, \dots$ such that $\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i = \Omega$.

The *indicator function* of $A$ is defined as $I_A(\omega) = I(\omega \in A) = \mathbf{1}_{\{\omega \in A\}}$.

A sequence of sets is *monotone increasing* if $A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq A_3 \subseteq \cdots$, and *monotone decreasing* if $A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq A_3 \supseteq \cdots$.

** Axioms of Probability

The *probability* of $A$ is a real number $\mathbb{P}(A)$ assigned to every event $A$, which satisfies the following three axioms:

1. *Non-negativity*: $\mathbb{P}(A) \ge 0$ for every $A$
2. *Normalization*: $\mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1$
3. *Countable additivity*: If $A_1, A_2, \dots$ are disjoint, then
   $$\mathbb{P}\!\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i \right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(A_i)$$

** Interpretations of Probability

There are two common interpretations of $\mathbb{P}(A)$: *frequencies* and *degrees of belief*.
 links statistics and probability under the frequency interpretation.

The difference in interpretation does not matter much until we deal with *statistical inference*, which gives rise to two schools: the *frequentist school* and the *Bayesian school* .

** Basic Properties

From the axioms, we can derive:

- \mathbb{P}(\varnothing) = 0$
- A \subset B \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbb{P}(A) \le \mathbb{P}(B)$
- 0 \le \mathbb{P}(A) \le 1$
- \mathbb{P}(A^c) = 1 - \mathbb{P}(A)$
- A \cap B = \varnothing \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbb{P}(A \cup B) = \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B)$

*Lemma*: For any events $A$ and $B$,
$$\mathbb{P}(A \cup B) = \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B) - \mathbb{P}(AB)$$

*Theorem* (Continuity of Probabilities):

This result depends only on Axiom 3. If $A_n \rightarrow A$, then $\mathbb{P}(A_n) \rightarrow \mathbb{P}(A)$ as $n \rightarrow \infty$.

** Uniform Probability Distribution

For a finite sample space $\Omega$ where each outcome is equally likely, the *uniform probability distribution* is given by
$$\mathbb{P}(A) = \frac{|A|}{|\Omega|}$$

This motivates the use of *combinatorial numbers* as counting tools.

The *binomial coefficient* "n choose k" is
$$\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$$

Important properties include:
- $$\binom{n}{0} = \binom{n}{n} = 1$$
- $$\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}$$

** Independence

Two events $A$ and $B$ are *independent* if
$$\mathbb{P}(AB) = \mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)$$
and we write $A \perp B$. If they are not independent, we write $A \not\!\perp B$.

Independence can arise in two distinct ways:
- Sometimes we explicitly *assume* that two events are independent.
- In other instances, we *derive* independence by verifying that $\mathbb{P}(AB) = \mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)$.

*Important conclusion*: Disjoint events with positive probability are /not/ independent.

** Conditional Probability

*Definition*: If $\mathbb{P}(B) > 0$, then the *conditional probability* of $A$ given $B$ is
$$\mathbb{P}(A \mid B) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(AB)}{\mathbb{P}(B)}$$

*** Prosecutor's Fallacy

The *prosecutor's fallacy* arises from confusing $\mathbb{P}(A \mid B)$ with $\mathbb{P}(B \mid A)$:

- "If you are innocent, the probability of seeing this evidence is extremely small."
- is incorrectly inferred as
- "Given this evidence, the probability that you are innocent is extremely small."

Note that $\mathbb{P}(\cdot \mid B)$ is a legal probability measure that satisfies the axioms of probability, but $\mathbb{P}(A \mid \cdot)$ does not satisfy the axioms as a function of its second argument.

*** Basic Results

*Lemma*: For any events $A$ and $B$ with $\mathbb{P}(B) > 0$,
$$\mathbb{P}(AB) = \mathbb{P}(A \mid B)\mathbb{P}(B) = \mathbb{P}(B \mid A)\mathbb{P}(A)$$

*The Law of Total Probability*: Let $\{A_1, \dots, A_k\}$ be a partition of $\Omega$. Then
$$\mathbb{P}(B) = \sum_{i=1}^k \mathbb{P}(B \mid A_i)\mathbb{P}(A_i)$$

*Bayes' Theorem*:
$$\mathbb{P}(A_i \mid B) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(B \mid A_i)\mathbb{P}(A_i)}{\sum_j \mathbb{P}(B \mid A_j)\mathbb{P}(A_j)}$$

The *prior probability* $\mathbb{P}(A_i)$ represents our judgment before seeing the data, while the *posterior probability* $\mathbb{P}(A_i \mid B)$ represents our updated judgment after observing the data.

** Measurable Spaces and Probability Spaces

Assigning probabilities to all subsets of $\Omega$ is not always feasible. Therefore, we restrict attention to a collection of events called a *σ-algebra* or *σ-field*.

A class $\mathcal{A}$ is a σ-algebra if it satisfies:

1. $\varnothing \in \mathcal{A}$: The impossible event must be measurable.
2. If $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{A}$, then $\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i \in \mathcal{A}$: Closed under countable unions.
3. $A \in \mathcal{A} \Rightarrow A^c \in \mathcal{A}$: Closed under complementation.

The sets in $\mathcal{A}$ are said to be *measurable*. We call $(\Omega, \mathcal{A})$ a *measurable space*.

If $\mathbb{P}$ is a *probability measure* defined on $\mathcal{A}$, then $(\Omega, \mathcal{A}, \mathbb{P})$ is called a *probability space*.

The *Borel σ-field* is a particularly important example of a σ-algebra in practice.
